# Module 8 · ETFs, funds and index investing

For most private clients, low-cost index ETFs form the core of the portfolio. Your edge comes from choosing the right exposure at the lowest all-in cost and knowing exactly what each fund owns.

## 8.1 Fund structures

| Structure | How it trades | Key features | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| ETF (exchange-traded fund) | Intraday on an exchange | Low cost, transparent holdings, tax-efficient | Bid-ask spreads; premiums or discounts in stressed markets |
| Mutual fund | Once a day at net asset value (NAV) | Automatic investing, many active managers | Capital-gains distributions; sales loads on some share classes |
| Index fund (ETF or mutual fund) | Either way | Tracks an index at minimal cost | Owns the index's flaws, such as concentration in the largest stocks |
| Active ETF | Intraday | Manager discretion in an ETF wrapper | Higher fees; must justify them |
| Closed-end fund | Intraday, often away from NAV | Fixed share count; frequently uses leverage | Discounts can widen in a sell-off |
| Exchange-traded note (ETN) | Intraday | Unsecured debt that promises an index return | Credit risk of the issuing bank |
| Money market fund | At a stable \$1 NAV (government funds) | Cash management | Yield follows the Fed down |

## 8.2 How ETFs work

- **Creation and redemption:** large dealers called authorized participants (APs) deliver a basket of securities to the fund in exchange for new ETF shares, or return shares for the basket.
- **Arbitrage:** if the ETF trades above the value of its holdings, APs create shares and sell them; if below, they buy and redeem. That keeps the price close to NAV.
- **Tax efficiency:** redemptions happen "in kind" (securities, not cash), so the fund rarely has to sell holdings at a gain. Many broad equity ETFs go years without distributing capital gains.
- **Stress:** when the underlying bonds trade less often than the ETF, as in March 2020, bond ETFs can trade at noticeable discounts. The ETF often becomes the price-discovery venue.

## 8.3 ETF analysis checklist

| What to check | Questions to ask | Where to find it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Objective and index | Which index? Weighted by market cap, equally, by fundamentals or by a factor? How often is it rebalanced? | Prospectus, index methodology |
| Holdings | How many? What share is in the top 10? Sector and country weights? Overlap with your other funds? | Issuer site, daily holdings file |
| Cost | Expense ratio, bid-ask spread, premium or discount to NAV | Issuer site, trading screen |
| Tracking | Tracking difference (fund return minus index return) and tracking error (its volatility) | Fact sheet, Morningstar |
| Liquidity | Average daily volume, but above all the liquidity of the underlying securities; fund assets | Issuer site, trading data |
| Risk | Volatility, beta, maximum drawdown; for bond ETFs, duration and credit quality | Fact sheet, Portfolio Visualizer |
| Income | Distribution yield and, for bond funds, the SEC 30-day yield | Fact sheet |
| Tax | Capital-gains distribution history, qualified dividends, K-1 forms for commodity pools | Issuer site, tax documents |
| Issuer | Fund size, sponsor quality, securities-lending policy, closure risk for very small funds | Issuer site |

## 8.4 Analyzing ETF growth and performance

- **Total return:** compare NAV return and market-price return over 1, 3, 5 and 10 years, annualized.
- **Rolling and calendar-year returns:** look at how the fund did in 2008, 2020 and 2022, not only in the trailing period that flatters it.
- **Risk-adjusted return:** Sharpe and Sortino ratios, maximum drawdown, and upside and downside capture against the benchmark ([Module 15](15-performance-measurement-and-reporting.md)).
- **What it owns:** weighted-average P/E, earnings growth, ROE and dividend growth of the holdings. A fund's future return comes from these, not from its past chart.
- **Dividend funds:** 5- and 10-year dividend growth rate, yield vs its own history, and payout ratios of the holdings.
- **Assets and flows:** growing assets signal liquidity and survival, not future returns. Popular funds often launch at the peak of a theme.

**Fees compound too.** \$100,000 earning 6% a year before costs for 30 years:

| Annual fee | Value after 30 years |
| --- | --- |
| 0.05% | \$566,277 |
| 1.00% | \$432,194 |

The 0.95-point difference costs \$134,083, nearly a quarter of the low-cost result. Costs are the most reliable predictor of fund performance you control.

## 8.5 Active vs passive

- **The arithmetic of active management (William Sharpe, 1991):** before costs, the average actively managed dollar must earn the market return, because together active investors hold the market. After costs, the average active dollar must therefore trail it.
- **The evidence:** S&P's SPIVA scorecards have shown year after year that most active US large-cap funds trail the S&P 500 over 10- and 15-year periods.
- **Where active can earn its fee:** less efficient markets (small caps, emerging markets, high-yield and municipal bonds), customized risk control, tax-loss harvesting, and clients with special needs such as ESG screens.
- **Core-satellite:** hold a low-cost index core (often 70–90%) and add satellites (individual stocks, active funds, factor tilts) where you have a reason. This structure works well in a competition: the core shows discipline and the satellites show research.

## 8.6 Factor and smart-beta ETFs

Factor ETFs tilt toward characteristics that research links to long-run excess returns or lower risk.

| Factor | What it buys | Why it might work | Fit for a conservative client |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Value | Cheap stocks (low P/E, P/B) | Risk premium or investor overreaction | Neutral; can lag for a decade |
| Size | Small companies | Compensation for risk and illiquidity | Low; more volatile |
| Momentum | Recent winners | Investors underreact to news | Low; sharp reversals |
| Quality | Profitable, low-debt, stable companies | Investors underpay for dull reliability | High |
| Minimum or low volatility | The least volatile stocks | Investors overpay for lottery-like stocks | High; usually falls less in sell-offs |
| Dividend growth | Companies with long records of raising dividends | Discipline and quality | High |

Factors can underperform for years, and crowded factors can crash. Choose a factor because it matches the client's risk profile, not because of its last three years. Tickers to research: QUAL (quality), USMV and SPLV (low volatility), VIG and DGRO (dividend growth), SCHD (dividend quality).

## 8.7 Specialized ETFs to handle with care

- **Leveraged and inverse ETFs** reset daily, so over time they drift away from their target multiple. If an index rises 10% and then falls 10%, it ends down 1%; a 2× fund ends down 4%, and a −1× inverse fund also ends down 1%. They are trading tools, not investments for a conservative client.
- **Thematic ETFs** (robotics, cannabis, space) are concentrated, often expensive and frequently launched near the peak of the theme.
- **Commodity futures ETFs** can lose money from rolling futures in contango, and some issue K-1 tax forms.
- **Buffered or defined-outcome ETFs** cap upside in exchange for absorbing a first slice of losses over a set period. They can suit a nervous client, but they cost more, and the protection resets with each outcome period.
- **Covered-call (option-income) ETFs** pay high distributions but give up much of the upside. Judge them on total return, not on yield.
- **Single-stock ETFs** add leverage or options to one company and carry concentrated risk.

## 8.9 Mutual fund share classes and costs

- **Loads:** front-end loads (Class A) charge when you buy; back-end loads (Class C or B) charge when you sell or over time; 12b-1 fees pay for marketing out of fund assets.
- **Prefer no-load, low-cost share classes.** Institutional and "admiral" classes are often cheaper versions of the same fund.
- **Total cost of ownership:** expense ratio + trading spread + tax drag from distributions + any advisory fee.

## Apply it to your case

- For each fund you pick, fill in the 8.3 checklist and keep it in your report's appendix.
- Explain each fund's role in one sentence ("VTIP protects Laura's purchasing power over the next five years with little rate risk").
- Check the overlap between funds so the portfolio is not secretly concentrated in the same ten mega-cap stocks.
