# Module 3 · Business management and strategy

Before you value a company, decide whether it is a good business: one that earns returns above its cost of capital and can defend them for years. For a conservative client, business quality is the first line of risk management, because great businesses recover from bad markets and weak ones often do not.

## 3.1 What makes a high-quality business

The thresholds below are rules of thumb; compare every company with its own industry.

| Trait | What to look for | Where to check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High returns on capital | Return on invested capital (ROIC) above the cost of capital, for example 12%+ against 8%, sustained for many years | 10-K history, data providers |
| Pricing power | Gross margins stable or rising, even through inflation | Gross margin trend |
| Recurring revenue | Subscriptions, long contracts, consumables | Revenue breakdown in the 10-K |
| Low capital intensity | Capex small relative to sales; most profit converts to free cash flow | Cash flow statement |
| Strong balance sheet | Net debt below about 2× EBITDA; operating profit covers interest 8× or more | Balance sheet, debt footnotes |
| Durable demand | Sales held up in 2008–09 and 2020 | Segment results in those years |
| Aligned management | Meaningful insider ownership; pay tied to per-share value and returns | Proxy statement (DEF 14A) |

## 3.2 Business models

| Model | How it makes money | Key metrics | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Subscription and software (SaaS) | Recurring fees | Annual recurring revenue, net revenue retention, churn, customer acquisition cost payback | High acquisition costs; dilution from stock-based pay |
| Marketplace or platform | A cut (take rate) of transactions | Gross merchandise value, take rate, active users | Regulation, being bypassed |
| Razor and blade | Cheap device, profitable refills | Installed base, attach rate | Cheaper copycat refills |
| Franchise | Royalties from franchisees | System-wide sales, unit growth, same-store sales | Franchisee financial health |
| Advertising | Sells audience attention | Users, engagement, revenue per user | Ad cycles, privacy rules |
| Licensing and royalties | Fees for intellectual property | Royalty rates, renewals | Patent expiry |
| Asset-heavy manufacturing | Sells physical goods | Capacity utilization, margins, inventory turns | Cyclical demand, heavy capex |
| Commodity producer | Sells at market prices | Cost per unit vs price, reserves | Price swings it cannot control |
| Regulated utility | An allowed return on its asset base | Rate-base growth, allowed return on equity | Regulators, interest rates |
| Bank or insurer | The spread on deposits, or float on premiums | Net interest margin, combined ratio, capital ratios | Credit losses, leverage |

## 3.3 Strategy frameworks

**Porter's Five Forces** tells you whether an industry lets companies keep their profits.

| Force | Question to ask | Favorable sign for investors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rivalry among competitors | Are price wars common? | A few rational competitors in a growing market |
| Threat of new entrants | How hard is it to start a rival? | High capital needs, regulation, brand, scale |
| Buyer power | Can customers force prices down? | Many small customers with high switching costs |
| Supplier power | Can suppliers squeeze margins? | Many suppliers of commodity inputs |
| Threat of substitutes | Could a different product do the same job? | No close substitute |

Other frameworks judges expect you to know:

- **SWOT:** internal strengths and weaknesses; external opportunities and threats.
- **PESTEL:** political, economic, social, technological, environmental and legal forces on an industry.
- **Porter's generic strategies:** cost leadership, differentiation, or focus on a niche. Companies "stuck in the middle" tend to earn poor returns.
- **Value chain:** primary activities (inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service) plus support activities. Find where the margin is created.
- **VRIO:** a resource gives lasting advantage if it is Valuable, Rare, hard to Imitate, and the firm is Organized to exploit it.
- **BCG growth-share matrix:** stars, cash cows, question marks and dogs, for companies with several segments.
- **Ansoff matrix:** market penetration, market development, product development and diversification, in rising order of risk.
- **Industry life cycle:** introduction, growth, shakeout, maturity, decline. Mature industries often suit income-focused clients.
- **Balanced Scorecard:** financial, customer, internal-process and learning-and-growth measures of performance.
- **Marketing mix (4Ps):** product, price, place and promotion.

## 3.4 Economic moats

A moat is a durable advantage that keeps competitors from eroding returns. Morningstar's framework names five sources:

| Moat source | How it works | Typical examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Intangible assets | Brands, patents and licenses that competitors cannot copy | Consumer brands, drug patents |
| Switching costs | Leaving is costly, risky or time-consuming for customers | Enterprise software, banking relationships |
| Network effects | Each new user makes the product more valuable | Payment networks, marketplaces |
| Cost advantage | Structurally lower costs from scale, process or location | Discount retailers, low-cost producers |
| Efficient scale | A market only big enough for one or a few players | Pipelines, airports, railroads |

Test a claimed moat with numbers: ROIC above the cost of capital for 10+ years, stable or rising market share, margins that held up in downturns, and price increases at or above inflation without losing customers. Moats erode through technology shifts and regulation, so ask what could breach this one.

## 3.5 Management and capital allocation

Management has five uses for cash: reinvest in the business, acquire companies, pay dividends, buy back shares or repay debt. Good capital allocators pick the option with the highest risk-adjusted return per dollar and explain why.

- **Judge the record:** compare past promises with results; read several years of shareholder letters and earnings calls.
- **Check alignment:** insider ownership, and pay tied to ROIC or per-share free cash flow rather than revenue or "adjusted" EPS.
- **Buybacks:** create value only when shares are bought below intrinsic value. Check whether the share count actually falls, or whether buybacks merely offset stock-based compensation.
- **Red flags:** serial acquisitions followed by goodwill write-downs; debt-funded buybacks at peak prices; "adjusted" metrics that exclude recurring costs; departures of the CFO or auditor; related-party deals; guidance that is always beaten by a cent.

## 3.6 Corporate governance

- **The agency problem:** managers (agents) may act in their own interest rather than shareholders' (principals). Governance exists to align them.
- **Board quality:** a majority of independent directors, an independent chair or lead director, relevant expertise and active audit, compensation and nominating committees.
- **Shareholder rights:** one share, one vote is best. Dual-class shares, staggered boards and poison pills entrench management.
- **Audit and controls:** a reputable auditor, a clean opinion, no material weaknesses, no restatements.
- **Where to look:** the proxy statement (DEF 14A) for pay and the board, the 10-K for risk factors and internal controls, and 8-K filings for sudden executive departures.

## 3.7 ESG and sustainable investing

ESG covers environmental (climate, emissions, resource use), social (labor, product safety, data privacy, communities) and governance (board, pay, shareholder rights, ethics) issues. Focus on issues that are financially material in that industry; the SASB standards, now maintained by the ISSB, map them industry by industry.

| Approach | What it does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Exclusionary screening | Removes industries or companies | No tobacco or weapons makers |
| ESG integration | Adds ESG risks to the financial analysis | Higher discount rate for poor governance |
| Best-in-class | Picks the ESG leaders within each sector | Top-rated utilities only |
| Thematic | Invests in a sustainability trend | Clean energy, water |
| Impact investing | Targets measurable social or environmental outcomes | Green bonds, community lending |
| Stewardship | Votes proxies and engages management | Pushing for an independent chair |

Pitfalls: ESG ratings from different providers often disagree, "greenwashing" overstates credentials, and exclusions shift sector weights away from the benchmark. In a competition, use ESG when the client states values or when an issue is financially material, and record it in the IPS under unique circumstances.

## 3.8 Corporate finance: how managers make decisions

- **Capital budgeting:** accept projects with a positive net present value (NPV) at a discount rate that matches their risk. The internal rate of return (IRR) must beat the hurdle rate, but beware projects of different sizes and cash flows that change sign more than once. Payback period is simple but ignores later cash flows.
- **Cost of capital:** the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) is the minimum return investors require ([Module 6](06-equity-valuation.md)).
- **Capital structure:** in a frictionless market, Modigliani–Miller says the debt-equity mix does not change firm value. In reality, trade-off theory balances the tax shield on interest against the costs of financial distress, and pecking-order theory says firms use internal cash first, then debt, then new equity.
- **Payout policy:** dividends are sticky and signal confidence; buybacks are flexible. Check that free cash flow covers the dividend.
- **Mergers and acquisitions:** acquirers usually pay a premium over market price, so value depends on synergies outweighing that premium and integration going smoothly. Many deals fail that test, so treat a big acquisition as a risk until the results prove otherwise.
- **Working capital:** the cash conversion cycle (days of receivables + days of inventory − days of payables) shows how long cash is tied up in operations.
- **Operating leverage:** the degree of operating leverage (percent change in EBIT ÷ percent change in sales) shows how much profits swing when sales swing.

## 3.9 Operations and performance management

- **KPIs and OKRs:** good companies report the same key performance indicators every year and explain misses. Changing metrics often is a warning sign.
- **Unit economics:** revenue and cost per unit or per customer; the lifetime value of a customer should comfortably exceed the cost of acquiring one.
- **Supply chain:** check for single-source suppliers, geographic concentration and the inventory strategy (just-in-time is efficient but fragile; just-in-case is resilient but ties up cash).
- **Execution:** compare management's targets with what was delivered, year after year.

## 3.10 Qualitative research checklist

1. How does the company make money, in one sentence?
2. Who are its customers, and why do they choose it?
3. What is the moat, and is it widening or narrowing?
4. How do the Five Forces look for its industry?
5. How did revenue and margins hold up in 2008–09 and 2020?
6. Does management allocate capital well, and does it own shares?
7. Is governance sound: board, auditor, shareholder rights?
8. What are the top three risks in the 10-K, and how likely are they?
9. Which ESG issues are material in this industry?
10. Why does this business belong in this client's portfolio?

## Apply it to your case

- For each stock candidate, write a one-paragraph business-quality summary covering the moat, the Five Forces and management.
- For a risk-averse client like Laura, favor wide-moat, low-debt, cash-generative businesses with steady dividends. Screen out companies that need everything to go right to work.
